Using the puzzle for reading practice

Sight words are the ones children need to recognize on sight, so hunting for them in a sea of letters is a nice change from flash cards.

  1. Read each word on the list aloud with your learner, using it in a short sentence.
  2. Ask them to find the word in the grid with a finger, then circle it.
  3. After the puzzle, point to each circled word and have them read it again.
  4. Celebrate each found word with a quick cheer or high five.

Variations to keep practice fresh

  • Sentence builder. Pick three words and write a sentence that uses all of them.
  • Color by sound. Circle words with a long vowel in blue and words with a tricky spelling in red.
  • Parent and child race. The parent and learner each solve their own copy and see who finishes first.

Tips for parents and teachers

Choose just ten or twelve words at a time. Overloading a struggling reader is more frustrating than helpful.

Look for words that are in the books your child reads at bedtime. Spotting them again in a story is the real reward of the puzzle.

Printing tips for early readers

Make letters easy to see by printing on plain white paper at full size, and use the larger lowercase option if your learner is just starting. Easy mode keeps every word running left to right or top to bottom.

Print two puzzles with different layouts so a child can try again the next day, and use the key only after the first attempt.

FAQ

Which sight word list is this?

It is a general mix of common early words. Match it to your school's list by editing the words above the puzzle.

Is lowercase better for young readers?

Books use mostly lowercase letters, so many teachers prefer it. Switch the case option to lowercase before pressing Generate.

How often should my child practice?

A short session of five to ten minutes a few times a week is usually enough. Stop when interest fades.
