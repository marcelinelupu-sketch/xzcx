How to use this word search in a space unit

Space names are tricky to spell, and a word search fixes them in the mind by repeating the letter patterns. It also gives students a reason to read every planet's name closely, rather than skimming a textbook.

  1. Start by naming the planets in order from the sun, using a rhyme or mnemonic your class likes.
  2. Solve the puzzle and ask students to write the planets in order beside the word list.
  3. Then find the other words, such as comet and orbit, and discuss what each one is.
  4. Finish with a question: which place would you most like to visit and why?

NASA's Solar System Exploration pages have photos and facts you can use to support the lesson.

Three ways to extend it

  • Planet fact cards. Each student picks one planet and writes three facts on an index card.
  • Scale walk. Use the planets as stops on a hallway model of the solar system, with distances marked by floor tiles or paces.
  • Astronaut interview. Students take turns as an astronaut returning from one planet, answering questions from classmates.

Tips for teachers and parents

The word Earth is short and easy to miss, which makes a fine lesson in search strategy. Suggest scanning for rarer letters, like the J in Jupiter or the U in Uranus, to spot long words faster.

Pair the puzzle with an evening of stargazing, or a look at the moon through binoculars, so the vocabulary connects to something real.

Printing tips for space lessons

Stay with white paper so letters stay crisp. Dark blue paper looks dramatic but hides pencil marks, unless you give children white gel pens.

The 14 by 14 grid fits Milky Way and asteroid comfortably. Laminate a class set and use dry-erase markers to reuse it across several days.
