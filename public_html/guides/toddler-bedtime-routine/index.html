How to Build a Toddler Bedtime Routine That Sticks

A bedtime routine is the same short series of calm steps, in the same order, every night. For toddlers, a picture chart makes those steps visible, so the routine becomes something they can follow instead of something they argue about. This guide covers how much sleep toddlers need, what to include, and how to keep it going.

Why routines help toddlers

This guide shares general parenting information, not medical advice. If you have concerns about your child's sleep, breathing, growth or behavior, talk to your pediatrician or family doctor.

Toddlers like to know what comes next. A predictable sequence at the end of the day gives them a sense of control, and repeating it night after night helps the steps themselves become a signal that sleep is coming. Research supports this. A large study led by sleep psychologist Jodi Mindell surveyed more than 10,000 mothers of children up to age five in fourteen countries and found that a consistent bedtime routine was associated with earlier bedtimes, falling asleep faster, fewer night wakings and longer sleep. The more nights a week families used the routine, the better the outcomes tended to be.

That study shows an association rather than proof that the routine alone caused better sleep, but it fits what many parents notice: a familiar routine makes the evening smoother for everyone.

How much sleep does a toddler need?

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine published recommended sleep amounts for children, and the American Academy of Pediatrics endorsed them. For toddlers aged one to two, the recommendation is 11 to 14 hours in each 24-hour period, including naps. From three to five years it is 10 to 13 hours, again including naps.

Recommended sleep per 24 hours by age, from the American Academy of Sleep Medicine
AgeRecommended sleep per 24 hours
4 to 12 months12 to 16 hours, including naps
1 to 2 years11 to 14 hours, including naps
3 to 5 years10 to 13 hours, including naps
6 to 12 years9 to 12 hours
13 to 18 years8 to 10 hours

Those are ranges, and healthy children vary within them. Work backward from the time your child usually wakes. If your two-year-old wakes at 6:30 in the morning and naps for two hours, a bedtime somewhere around 7:00 to 8:00 in the evening puts total sleep inside the recommended range. Watch your own child too: a toddler who is regularly cranky in the late afternoon, or falls asleep in the car at every chance, may need an earlier bedtime.

What to include

Keep it short and simple, with a clear ending. Four to six steps is plenty for a toddler. The American Academy of Pediatrics promotes an easy-to-remember routine for young children called Brush, Book, Bed: brush teeth, read together, and go to bed at the same time each night. It is a good backbone that you can build around.

A sample toddler routine might look like this:

  1. Bath or a quick wash
  2. Pajamas on
  3. Brush teeth
  4. Potty or a fresh diaper, then wash hands
  5. One or two books in bed or in a cozy chair
  6. Cuddle, a song or a short goodnight phrase, then lights off

Every family's version will be different. Some do the bath only on certain nights. Some add a drink of water early in the routine, or a goodnight to the family pet. What matters is that the steps happen in the same order, in the same calm way, every night.

The steps toward the end of the routine should be the quietest. Rough play, tickling and exciting games work better earlier in the evening, before the routine starts.

Screens and light

The AAP recommends avoiding screens for about an hour before bed and keeping TVs, tablets and phones out of children's bedrooms. Bright, exciting content right before bed can make settling down harder. Moving screen time earlier, and making the last hour of the day about books, songs and quiet play, gives the routine a calmer run-up. Dim lights during the final steps help signal that the day is ending.

Why a picture chart helps

Most toddlers cannot read, but they understand pictures very well. A picture chart shows each step as a big, simple icon in order, so your child can see where they are in the routine and what comes next. It turns "time for bed" from an instruction into a shared plan you both follow.

Charts also give toddlers a small job. Many enjoy pointing to the next picture, moving a clip down the chart or putting a sticker beside each step. That sense of being in charge often reduces the push-back around steps they dislike, such as brushing teeth.

Making it stick

Start the same time each night

Begin the routine at roughly the same time every evening, including weekends where you can. The routine works best when it leads to a consistent bedtime.

Build in the requests you know are coming

If your toddler always asks for water, another hug or one more story, put those things into the routine. A drink of water before teeth, and exactly two books, is easier to hold to than a negotiation after lights out.

Offer small choices

Toddlers want some control. Let them choose between two pairs of pajamas or which of two books to read. The routine itself stays fixed; the small choices give them a say inside it.

Keep the ending boring

Once the light goes off, keep any response short, quiet and the same each time. A calm, brief goodnight is easier to repeat than a long conversation.

Expect some bumps

Illness, travel, a new sibling or a move can all upset sleep for a while. Keep the chart and as many steps as you can, even in a hotel room or at a grandparent's house. Familiar steps in an unfamiliar place can be especially comforting.

Using rewards carefully

Some families add a sticker for each night the routine goes smoothly, especially with older toddlers and preschoolers. Keep rewards small and immediate, and focus them on the steps your child controls, like putting on pajamas, rather than on falling asleep quickly, which is harder to will. FAQ

How long should a toddler bedtime routine take?

Long enough to include each step calmly, and short enough to repeat every night. Many families settle on four to six steps. If the routine regularly runs long, look for steps that can move earlier in the evening.

At what age should I start a bedtime routine?

You can start early. The AAP's Brush, Book, Bed approach is designed for young children, and adding a picture chart works well once your toddler can point to pictures.

How many hours of sleep does a two-year-old need?

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends 11 to 14 hours in every 24 hours, including naps, for children aged one to two. From three to five, the range is 10 to 13 hours, including naps.

Is this medical advice?

No. It is general information for parents. If your child snores loudly, seems very tired during the day, or you are worried about their sleep for any reason, speak to your pediatrician.
