75-Ball vs 90-Ball Bingo Explained

75-ball bingo is the American game, played on a 5 by 5 card with a free center and a new winning pattern each round. 90-ball bingo is the British game, played on tickets of three rows where every game pays out for one line, two lines and a full house. Here is how each one works and how to pick between them.

The short answer

Both games use a caller who draws numbered balls at random and players who mark matching numbers. The differences are in the card, the number range and what counts as a win. The table sums it up, and the sections below explain the details that matter when you are the one running the game.

How 75-ball bingo works

A 75-ball card has five columns headed B, I, N, G and O. Each column draws its five numbers from a fixed band of fifteen: B holds 1 to 15, I holds 16 to 30, N holds 31 to 45 (with the free space in the middle, so only four numbers), G holds 46 to 60 and O holds 61 to 75. That makes 24 numbers per card. Because every number belongs to exactly one column, the caller announces the letter as well, as in "G 52", which tells players exactly where to look.

The column bands also make cards easy to check. If someone claims a win with 44 under the B, the card is either misprinted or the claim is wrong. When you print cards from our 75-ball card page, the generator keeps every number in its correct column automatically.

The defining feature of 75-ball is the pattern. Before each game the caller names a target shape, such as a single line, four corners, a letter T or a full blackout, and only that shape wins. A session usually mixes quick and slow patterns to vary the pace. Our bingo patterns gallery draws more than a dozen of them and explains roughly how long each one takes.

How 90-ball bingo works

A 90-ball ticket has three rows and nine columns. Each row holds exactly five numbers and four blanks, so a ticket has fifteen numbers in all. The columns are banded by tens: the first column takes 1 to 9, the second 10 to 19, and so on up to the last, which takes 80 to 90. Every column on a ticket has at least one number, and numbers in a column run from smallest at the top to largest at the bottom.

Tickets are usually printed in strips of six. A correctly made strip uses every number from 1 to 90 exactly once across its six tickets, so a player holding a full strip marks something on every single call. If you do the arithmetic, the first column of a strip holds nine numbers, the last holds eleven, and each of the others holds ten. Building strips by hand is fiddly, which is why our 90-ball ticket generator does it for you and checks each strip.

Instead of a different pattern each game, one 90-ball game pays out three times. The first prize goes to the first player to complete any one horizontal line on a ticket. Play continues for two lines on the same ticket, then continues again until someone covers all fifteen numbers, which is called a full house. In halls the full house is the big prize of the game.

The calls

In 90-ball the caller says only the number, since there are no column letters. British halls developed a tradition of rhyming or jokey nicknames for many numbers, such as "Kelly's eye" for 1 and "legs eleven" for 11. They are optional, but they add a lot of charm to a party game, and our online bingo caller has an optional set of family-friendly traditional calls for 90-ball games.

Which game should you play?

If you are in North America, or your players learned bingo at school, choose 75-ball. Everyone already knows the card, the letter calls make it easy for beginners and children to find their numbers, and you can change the pattern whenever the room needs a quick win or a long finale. It is also the natural shape for word bingo, which is why every themed card on this site uses a 5 by 5 (or smaller) grid.

If your players grew up in the UK or Ireland, choose 90-ball. The three-stage payout keeps people engaged for the whole game, because even after the first line is gone there are two more prizes to chase. It suits fundraisers, community nights and family gatherings where each game can run a little longer.

A few practical differences can tip the decision:

  • Time per game. Hosting 90-ball means three wins per game, so you need fewer separate games to hand out the same number of prizes. 75-ball lets you control the length through the pattern you choose.
  • Number of players. With 90-ball strips, each player can hold six tickets that cover every number. For 75-ball, players who want more action simply take two or three cards.
  • Age. Young children find 75-ball easier. The letter tells them which column to scan, and a single line is a fast, satisfying win. For very young groups our 1 to 30 number bingo is gentler still.
  • Paper. A 75-ball card fills a half or quarter page nicely. 90-ball tickets are wide and short, and six of them stack neatly down an A4 or Letter sheet.

Other bingo formats you may meet

Online bingo sites run several other versions, including 80-ball on a 4 by 4 grid, and fast 30-ball games on a 3 by 3 grid. In some countries a 90-number party game called housie or tambola uses the same three-row tickets as British bingo. At home, though, the two classic formats cover nearly every need, and word bingo is simply 75-ball bingo with words in place of numbers. Encyclopaedia Britannica lists many other names for the game, including lotto, housy-housy and tombola, which shows how widely it has traveled.

Running either game at home

  1. Print one card or ticket per player, plus a few spares for latecomers. Our bingo card generator makes up to 30 unique cards at a time in either format.
  2. Decide on prizes. For 90-ball, plan three per game. For 75-ball, plan one per pattern.
  3. Use a caller you can trust. Pulling numbers from a bowl works, or open our bingo caller on a laptop or projector, which keeps a board of called numbers for checking claims.
  4. Explain the win before the first call. In 75-ball, name and show the pattern. In 90-ball, remind players that the first prize is any single row.
  5. Check every claim against the called numbers before announcing a winner. Our bingo rules guide covers ties and mistaken calls.

Tip: Coins, dried beans and dabber pens all work as markers, but if you want to reuse cards, slip them into clear sheet protectors and mark with dry-erase pens.

FAQ

Why does 75-ball bingo have a free space?

The free center square gives every player one mark before the first call, so lines through the middle need only four numbers. It also makes the card's 24 numbers fit neatly around the center of the 5 by 5 grid.

How many numbers are on a 90-ball ticket?

Fifteen. Each of the three rows has five numbers and four blank squares, spread across nine columns.

Does a strip of six 90-ball tickets really use every number?

It should. A proper strip uses each number from 1 to 90 exactly once across its six tickets, so the holder marks a number on every call. Our generator builds strips that follow this rule.

Can I play 90-ball bingo with word cards?

Not easily, because the ticket layout depends on number ranges. For words, use the 75-ball style grid with a single line, pattern or blackout win.
