How to Plan a Scavenger Hunt Kids and Adults Will Love

A good scavenger hunt needs four decisions made in advance: who is playing, where the boundaries are, how long it lasts and how it will be scored. Get those right and the list almost writes itself. This guide walks through each step, with sample timings and safety rules for outdoor hunts.

Step 1: Decide who, where and how long

Start with the players. Toddlers need a handful of large, familiar things within arm's reach of an adult. School-age children can handle a list of twenty or more and a larger space. Teens and adults enjoy harder items, photo challenges and a bit of competition. Write down the youngest player's age and plan for them; older players can always be given bonus items.

Next, the space. A hunt can happen in a single living room, a backyard, a classroom, a park or along a walking route. Walk the area yourself beforehand. Note what is actually there, because a list that includes a pine cone in a park with no pine trees creates frustration, not fun.

Finally, the time. For young children, fifteen to twenty minutes is usually plenty. Older kids and adults can go for thirty to forty-five minutes. Decide on a firm end time and a signal, such as a whistle or a group text, that tells everyone to return.

Step 2: Choose a format

  • Checklist hunt. Players tick items off as they find them. Simple, fast to set up and good for any age.
  • Collecting hunt. Players bring items back in a bag. Fun indoors and for small objects, but outdoors it can mean picking things that should be left alone, so prefer the checklist there.
  • Photo hunt. Players photograph each item, which works for teens and adults with phones and for things that cannot be collected, like a red door or a dog on a leash.
  • Clue trail. Each clue leads to the next location. It takes more preparation and only one team can follow a trail at a time unless you create several routes.
  • Team score sheet. Items carry points, and teams decide which ones to chase. Ideal when groups are competitive or the space is large.

Step 3: Write a balanced list

The list is the heart of the hunt. A good one has a mix of easy wins, a middle band of items that take a bit of searching, and a few hard ones that separate the teams. A useful shape is roughly half easy, a third medium and the rest hard. Easy items keep younger players motivated in the first few minutes; hard items keep fast teams busy until time is called.

Write each item so there is no argument about whether it counts. "Something round" leads to debate, while "something round and smaller than your hand" is clear. For photo hunts, say exactly what must be in the picture: "your whole team with a statue" rather than "a statue".

Add variety by mixing kinds of item:

  • Things to spot, such as a bird, a stop sign or a flag.
  • Things to find by sense, such as something that smells sweet or something rough.
  • Things to do, such as hop on one foot for ten seconds or sing a line of a song.
  • Things to count, such as how many windows are on the front of the building.

Step 4: Set boundaries and safety rules

Tell players exactly where they may go, and show them if you can. Name the boundaries out loud: "the fence, the big oak and the path". For any hunt outside a home, every child should be with an adult or an older buddy, and young children should stay in sight of their grown-up at all times.

Make a few rules non-negotiable and say them before the start:

  • No running near roads, water or parking lots.
  • No going into other people's yards or buildings.
  • Leave plants, nests and animals alone. Look, photograph and tick, but do not pick or disturb.
  • Come back at the signal, even if the list is unfinished.
  • If anyone gets separated, stay where you are and wait to be found.

Leaving natural things where they are is one of the seven Leave No Trace principles, and it protects the place for the next visitor. In parks and reserves some plants are protected, so a checklist or photo format is always the better choice outdoors. Ready-made outdoor lists, such as our nature walk hunt, park hunt and backyard hunt, are written to be spotted rather than collected.

Pack a small kit for outdoor hunts: water, sunscreen, a basic first aid kit, a charged phone and the list of players with a contact number for each group.

Step 5: Form teams and print materials

Teams of two to four work best. Larger teams leave some players with nothing to do. Mix ages within each team so younger children are supported, and give each team a clipboard or a sturdy piece of cardboard to write on, plus a pencil (pens fail in the rain).

Print one list per team and a spare. If several teams start at the same time, print each copy with a shuffled order so they spread out instead of following one another. Write the team name and the end time at the top of each sheet. For long outdoor hunts, a sheet protector keeps the list dry and readable.

Step 6: Run it and score it

Gather everyone, read the rules, show the boundaries and confirm the end signal. Start all teams together. During the hunt, the organizer should stay at the base or walk the area, not join a team, so someone is always available for questions or emergencies.

When time is called, collect the sheets and score them together. Count one point per item, or use the points you set for harder finds. Decide ties in advance: the team that returned first, a quick bonus challenge, or simply shared first place. With young children, celebrate every team for something, such as the funniest photo or the best teamwork.

A short debrief makes the ending feel special. Ask teams to share the hardest item, the strangest find or the best photo. It gives slower teams a moment in the spotlight and often becomes the part everyone remembers.

Sample timings

  • Toddlers, indoors: 8 to 12 big, familiar items, about 10 to 15 minutes, with an adult walking alongside.
  • Ages 5 to 8, backyard or park: 15 to 20 items, about 20 minutes, in pairs with adult helpers.
  • Ages 9 to 12, neighborhood or campsite: 20 to 30 items, 30 to 40 minutes, in teams with set boundaries and an adult per group.
  • Teens and adults, photo hunt: 25 to 40 items with points, 45 minutes or more.

These are starting points, not rules. Watch the first few minutes. If teams are finishing too fast, add a bonus list; if they are stuck, read out a hint or two.

FAQ

How many items should a scavenger hunt have?

About 10 for toddlers, 15 to 20 for young children, and 20 to 40 for older kids and adults. The right number depends on the time you have and how spread out the items are.

How do I stop teams from following each other?

Print each team's list in a different order, start teams in different spots, or use points so teams choose different targets. Our generator can shuffle the order on every copy.

Is it okay for children to collect things outdoors?

It is better to tick or photograph items instead. Picking plants or moving natural objects can harm the place, and some plants in parks and reserves are protected.
