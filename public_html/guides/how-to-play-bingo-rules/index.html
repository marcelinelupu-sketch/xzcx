How to Play Bingo, From Setup to Checking a Win

Bingo is simple: a caller announces numbers or words at random, players mark them on their cards, and the first person to complete the agreed pattern shouts "Bingo!" and wins. The rules below cover the details that cause arguments at home games, from checking a claim to settling a tie.

What you need

  • Cards. One per player, each different. Number cards follow the 75-ball or 90-ball layout; word cards hold themed words in a grid. Our bingo card generator prints up to 30 unique cards in one go.
  • Markers. Bingo daubers, crayons, coins, buttons, cereal pieces or small paper squares. Pens work if each card is used once.
  • Something to call from. A bowl of numbered slips, a set of bingo balls, the caller checklist that prints with our cards, or the free online bingo caller.
  • A way to track calls. The caller needs a record of everything already called, so claims can be checked. A printed checklist or the caller's on-screen board does the job.
  • Prizes, if you are playing for them. Small treats, stickers, or the right to choose the next pattern all work. Charging players for cards or offering cash prizes can turn bingo into regulated gaming in many countries and states, so check your local rules before you do.

Setting up a game

Hand out cards and markers before you explain anything, because people listen better once they are holding something. If you are playing number bingo, tell everyone which format it is. In 75-ball games the caller says a letter and a number, while in 90-ball games only the number is called. Our guide to 75-ball and 90-ball bingo explains the two layouts.

Then announce what wins. In most home and classroom games this is a single line: any complete row, column or diagonal. You can also play four corners, a letter shape, or a blackout where every square must be covered. Show the pattern as well as saying it; a quick sketch on a whiteboard prevents a lot of disputes. The bingo patterns gallery has pictures of the common ones.

Finally, mention the free space. On a 5 by 5 card the center square is usually free, so every player marks it straight away. If you are playing a game where the free space does not count, say so now.

The rules, step by step

  1. The caller draws one number or word at random and reads it aloud clearly, then reads it a second time.
  2. Every player checks their card. Anyone who has the item marks it. Players without it do nothing.
  3. The caller records the item as called, either by setting the ball aside, ticking the checklist, or letting the online caller log it.
  4. The caller pauses long enough for everyone to look, then draws the next item.
  5. When a player completes the announced pattern, they call out "Bingo!" right away, before the next item is drawn.
  6. The caller stops drawing and checks the claim against the record of called items.
  7. If every marked square in the pattern was called, the claim is good and that player wins the round. If not, play continues from where it stopped.
  8. For the next round, players clear their cards, or take fresh ones, and the caller returns every item to the pool.

Encyclopaedia Britannica notes that in many places the winner shouts "house" instead of "bingo", which is common in British halls. Either word is fine at home, as long as everyone agrees which one counts.

How to call a good game

A good caller makes a game fair and fun. Speak more slowly than feels natural, especially with children or a noisy room. Say each item twice, and for numbers it helps to split the digits on the second read, such as "forty-seven, four seven", so that 47 and 74 are not confused. In 75-ball games, always include the letter.

Keep a steady rhythm. If players are still searching when you draw the next item, slow down; if the room is chatting and waiting, speed up. When you call words rather than numbers, you can read a definition, show a picture, or act the word out instead of saying it, which turns the game into a quick review for a class. Our classroom bingo ideas collect several ways to do this.

If you are both hosting and calling, an online caller with a large display saves your voice and keeps a visible board of everything called, which settles most "did you call that?" questions instantly.

Claiming and checking a win

A claim counts only if the player calls out before the next item is drawn. This is the most important house rule to agree on in advance. Most bingo halls treat a win that is spotted late as missed, and many families play the same way to keep players paying attention. With young children it is kinder to allow a late claim up until the next player wins.

To check a claim, ask the player to read out only the marked squares that make up the pattern. Compare each one with your called list. Check every square, even when the player is confident, and do it out loud so the room can follow. If one item was not called, the claim fails. Thank the player, ask them to remove the wrong mark, and carry on calling.

Ties

Two players sometimes complete the pattern on the same call. The fairest options are to share the prize, give each a prize if you have spares, or use a quick tiebreaker. A common tiebreaker is for each tied player to draw one more number from the pool, with the higher number winning. Choose one method before the game, so nobody feels it was invented on the spot.

Mistakes and false calls

Mistakes happen, especially with children and word cards that use similar words. A false claim usually has no penalty at home; the player simply keeps playing. If the caller misreads or skips an item, correct it as soon as it is noticed and announce the correction to everyone.

Rules for word bingo

Word bingo follows the same steps with a few small differences. The caller reads words instead of numbers, so there is no letter column to help players search, and long lists take a little longer to scan. Smaller grids, such as 3 by 3 or 4 by 4, keep the pace up for younger players. If your word list is shorter than the number of squares on a card, some words will repeat across a card, which can make checking confusing, so it is better to add words or choose a smaller grid.

For party games such as baby shower or bridal shower bingo, the items are often called by events rather than a caller. Guests mark a gift word whenever that gift is opened, and the first full line wins. Agree in advance whether a similar gift counts. A knitted hat probably counts as "beanie"; a gift card probably does not count as "blanket".

Friendly house rules for children

Bingo works beautifully with young children, but strict rules can lead to tears. A few changes keep it fun:

  • Play to a single line, so rounds finish quickly.
  • Let the winner become the next caller, which spreads the excitement around the room.
  • Play until several players have won, not just the first one.
  • Use stickers or a high five instead of prizes, or let the winner choose the next pattern.
  • For the very youngest, try a picture-free 3 by 3 card with short, familiar words, or our number bingo for 1 to 30.

Whatever rules you choose, the key is to state them before the first call and apply them the same way to everyone. That alone prevents nearly all bingo arguments.

FAQ

Do you have to shout bingo before the next number is called?

In most games, yes. A claim is accepted only if the player calls out before the next item is drawn. At home you can relax this rule for children, as long as you say so at the start.

What happens if two people get bingo at the same time?

If both completed the pattern on the same call, both are winners. Share the prize, give two prizes, or have each player draw a number and let the higher one win.

Can the caller play too?

It is better if they do not, since the caller controls the draw and is also the person checking claims. In a small family game nobody minds, but at a party or fundraiser keep the caller neutral.

How many cards can each player have?

As many as they can follow comfortably. Children usually manage one card. Experienced adults often play two or three, and in 90-ball bingo a strip of six tickets is common.
