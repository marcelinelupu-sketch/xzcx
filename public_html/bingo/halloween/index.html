How to play Halloween bingo

Halloween parties go from calm to wild in minutes, so bingo works best as the anchor activity at the start or end, when children need to sit and a grown-up needs a minute to breathe. It also suits a front step after trick-or-treating, when everyone sorts candy and wants a game that uses their loot.

  1. Give each player a card and a small pile of markers. Candy corn, pumpkin seeds, plastic spiders and orange paper circles all fit the theme. Wrapped candy is tempting but gets eaten, so save it for prizes.
  2. Call squares in a spooky voice, or hold up props such as a rubber bat or a small pumpkin for younger players.
  3. Players cover each match, and the first with a complete line shouts "Boo!" or says the party's secret word.
  4. Verify the covered squares, then let the winner choose from the prize bowl.
  5. Clear the cards and begin again with the next caller, who might be a witch, a ghost or a dinosaur in costume.

Each round can last under ten minutes, which suits a school party with several other stations.

Spooky variations

Costume contest bingo

Draw squares from the costumes in the room. Guests cover a square when they spot a costume that matches, such as a pirate, a witch or a superhero. It encourages mingling and ends with a natural moment for awards.

Haunted walk

Take the cards on a lit neighborhood stroll. Children tick off squares as they spot porch pumpkins, cobwebs and glowing windows. Stay together and carry flashlights, and keep to the sidewalk.

Mystery potion bingo

Fill a cauldron with small objects in the shapes of squares, such as a rubber spider or a plastic bat. Children pull out an object in turn, and anyone with the matching square covers it.

Quiet classroom version

For teachers who want a calm break from sugar, use the 3x3 grid and restrict squares to nature items such as an owl, a pumpkin and a full moon.

Tips for a smooth party

Think about the range of ages. Preschoolers prefer cartoonish, funny items such as a friendly monster and a pumpkin face, while older children like a bit of a shiver. You can print two lists and offer a gentle card and a spookier card.

Allergies matter at Halloween. Use non-food markers and prizes when you can, and ask families about nut allergies before the event. Teal pumpkin treats, such as stickers and small toys, are an inclusive choice that many families appreciate.

Keep the lights on. A dim room is fun, but children cannot see the squares in the dark, so use string lights or lanterns near the tables.

Use a free space for a surprise, like a bat sticker on the card that the child can add themselves. It gives a small win to every player at the start.

Printing for the party

Black and orange paper looks great but hides the text, so print on white or pale yellow and let the colors come from your decorations. Turn on ink saver if you want a plain black grid with less ink.

Choose two cards per page for kindergarten and first grade, and four per page for older children. If you have a big group, print the caller sheet on one sheet and read from it so you do not have to draw slips.

Cardstock stands up to sticky candy fingers. A sheet protector turns a card into a wipe-clean game, and a marker works on it as well as a counter.

FAQ

Is Halloween bingo too scary for little ones?

No. The squares are meant to be playful and silly. You can edit the list to remove anything you find too spooky, and use words like friendly ghost or pumpkin face for the youngest players.

Can I print cards with pictures instead of words?

The generator uses words, so for young children print the card and let them draw a small picture in each square before play, or read the words aloud as you point to props.

What can we use as markers if candy is not allowed?

Paper pumpkins, pennies, dried beans, buttons or small stickers all work. Many schools prefer non-food markers so that everyone can take part safely.

How do I run bingo while kids are trick-or-treating?

Give each child a card in their bag and have them tick squares as they spot things on the walk. At the end of the night, compare cards by the light of the porch and pick winners.
