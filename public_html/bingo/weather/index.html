How to play weather bingo

Weather bingo works in two ways. You can call words in class, or you can play it live by watching the sky across a week and covering squares as conditions appear.

  1. Give each child a card and some counters, such as cotton balls or paper clouds.
  2. For the classroom game, the caller describes a condition, like water falling in tiny drops, and players find drizzle.
  3. For the live game, hang the card near the window and cover squares as weather arrives.
  4. A row of covered squares earns a prize or a choice of seat.
  5. At the end of the week, discuss which squares stayed empty and why.

Three ways to extend the game

  • Cloud spotter round. Take cards outside and cover cumulus, stratus and cirrus squares only when you can match the sky to a chart.
  • Season sort. After playing, have children mark each square with a sun, leaf, snowflake or flower to show when it is most common.
  • Forecast challenge. Each morning, players predict which squares might be covered by afternoon and check afterward.

Tips for teachers

Keep a simple weather log beside the card. Dates and short notes turn the game into data for a bar graph.

Use the word storm carefully with children who find thunder frightening. Talk about safety rules at the same time, such as going indoors when thunder rumbles.

The National Weather Service site at weather.gov has pictures of cloud types you can show on a screen.

Printing for the window sill

Print on light blue or gray paper and tape the card where students can reach it. Ink saver keeps the lines light so pencil marks stand out.

If you plan to reuse the card all season, laminate it and use a dry-erase marker, then wipe clean at the start of each week.

FAQ

Can I play weather bingo outdoors?

Yes. Take the card outside and cover squares such as wind, cloud type and puddle only when you see them.

Do I need to know weather terms to run it?

No. The list includes simple words and a few technical ones, and the tips above point to a source for the definitions.

What age is this suitable for?

Kindergarteners can play with sunny, rainy and snowy squares. Third graders can manage the full list with cloud names.
