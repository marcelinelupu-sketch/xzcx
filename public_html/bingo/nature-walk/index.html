How to play on a walk

A walk with a bingo card changes how children move. Instead of rushing to the end of the trail they stop, crouch and look for the next square. Hand out cards at the trailhead and walk at the pace of the slowest searcher.

  1. Each walker gets a card, a pencil and a clipboard or stiff folder.
  2. Cover a square only when you have seen the item with your own eyes. Photos on the way home do not count.
  3. Look and leave. Nothing is picked, dug up or carried out of the woods.
  4. Call out a find so others can come over and share it.
  5. Stop at a picnic spot to compare cards and decide who got a full line.

Three ways to vary the walk

  • Sit spot round. Stop in a quiet corner for ten minutes and cover only squares you can spot without moving.
  • Sound bingo. Make a version with calls and rustles, such as birdsong, wind in leaves and running water, and play with eyes closed.
  • Sketch card. Draw each find in its square instead of covering it, which gives a nature journal page when the walk ends.

Tips for walk leaders

Choose the list to match the season. Fall cards lean on acorns, leaves and mushrooms, while spring cards lean on nests, blossoms and puddles. Edit before printing.

Bring a small magnifying glass and a field guide for the older kids. Naming what they find turns the game into a quiet science lesson.

Talk about safety up front: stay in sight, avoid touching unknown plants, and keep hands off wild animals and mushrooms.

Printing for muddy weather

A card in a zip-top bag survives drizzle. Print on lighter cardstock so it folds into a pocket without tearing.

For a school group, print four per page, cut the sheet and slip them onto a lanyard. Use ink saver so the small print stays crisp outdoors.

FAQ

Can I use nature walk bingo in a city park?

Yes. Most squares are common in parks, like squirrels, clover and robins, so a short loop around a pond or a tree-lined block works well.

Is it okay to pick up the items on the card?

Better not to. Looking and photographing keep the woods intact, though fallen acorns and leaves are fine to examine and put back.

What ages are best?

Preschoolers can join with a grown-up reading the squares. Kids from six to ten manage alone, and older ones enjoy the sketch version.
