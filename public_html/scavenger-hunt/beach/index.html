How to play at the beach

A beach hunt gives children something to do between swims and stops the 'I'm bored' that tends to arrive about forty minutes after the sunscreen. Start it once you have set up your spot, so children have a base to return to.

  1. Look at the list on arrival and tell children which items depend on the tide, like tide pools and shells.
  2. Give each child or team a sheet. A clip-on bag or small tote keeps it from blowing away.
  3. Let children roam within sight of your umbrella. Mark the edge of the search area with a towel or a bucket at each end.
  4. Rather than collecting finds, ask children to tell you or take a photo, then put things back.
  5. Meet back at the towels for a snack and compare lists before the next swim.

Three ways to vary the beach hunt

  • Photo hunt. Choose the photo layout, and have older children shoot each find on a waterproof camera or a phone in a zip bag. Afterward, make a slideshow for the car ride home.
  • Sandcastle edition. Add items they must build, like a tower with a flag or a bridge across a moat. It turns the list into a building contest.
  • Tide chart. Run the hunt twice, once at high tide and once at low, and compare what shows up. It is a nice way to talk about how the sea moves.

Tips for beach days

Print the list in a larger size by choosing the single-column checklist if you are bringing small children, since sun glare makes small type difficult to read.

Give a prize for the most interesting find rather than the most finds. It rewards patient looking and keeps the contest friendly between siblings of different ages.

Take a short shell-and-sand break whenever the hunt stalls. Children often rediscover the list on their own.

Beach safety

Keep children within arm's reach of an adult near the water, and swim only where there is a lifeguard if possible. Check the flags and heed local warnings about currents, tides and weather.

Do not touch jellyfish, even stranded ones, and leave any dead animal, broken glass or unfamiliar object for an adult to deal with. Use sunscreen, hats and plenty of water, and respect dunes and nesting birds. Many beaches ask visitors not to take shells, so check local rules.

Printing for sand and sun

Sand, sunscreen and water are hard on paper. Print on cardstock, or slip each sheet into a zip-top bag and tick with a marker through the plastic. Pick the black-and-white mode, since the high contrast helps in bright light. A4 or Letter works fine, and a beach bag fits a folded sheet comfortably.

FAQ

Can we collect shells on the beach hunt?

The list is made for spotting and photographing. Rules vary by beach and country, and leaving shells in place protects the small creatures that use them as homes.

What if our beach has no tide pools?

Delete the items that do not fit and add ones that do. A lakeshore might offer driftwood and dragonfly spotting, while a busy town beach may suit sandcastles and boats.

Is this good for toddlers?

Toddlers can join with an adult reading the list and pointing. A short list of six or eight easy items like a shell, a gull and something blue is plenty.
