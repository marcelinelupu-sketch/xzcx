Games that get students talking

ESL bingo is the center of the set. A student or the teacher calls a word, and learners find it on their card. Once the group is confident, ask students to take the caller's role. Describing a word without saying it is excellent speaking practice.

A word search helps with spelling. Learners see each word in print and have to read letter by letter, which suits students who speak well but struggle to write.

A word scramble pushes spelling further. Use a word bank at first, then take it away for confident groups.

For action verbs, try the scavenger hunt format: students find an object and say what they do with it.

The kitchen and fruits and vegetables word searches pair well with the food bingo cards, and the classroom scavenger hunt gets new arrivals moving around the room while they learn the names of things they will use every day.

Adapting the games to your class

Keep each list short for beginners. Twelve to sixteen words is enough for a first lesson, and the bingo card generator will make a smaller card to match.

For mixed ability groups, pair a stronger student with a beginner and let them share a card. The stronger student reads aloud and the beginner points.

Our guide to ESL vocabulary games has lesson ideas, and you can type your own course words into any tool. Everything is free, and no sign-up is needed.

Keep a record of which lists you used with which class. Students enjoy a rematch with a harder version, and a list that has been played once is far easier for them to read the second time.

FAQ

What level are the ESL printables for?

The lists are aimed at beginners and elementary learners. Because you can edit every word, you can raise the level by adding longer or harder vocabulary.

Can adults use these ESL games?

Yes. Adult classes often enjoy bingo and word puzzles too. Change the words to suit your students' goals.

How do I use these games with a class that shares no common language?

Show each word with a picture or an action the first time you call it, then let the cards do the rest. Bingo works well here because students only need to recognize the word, not explain it, and they can copy the caller's pronunciation round after round.
